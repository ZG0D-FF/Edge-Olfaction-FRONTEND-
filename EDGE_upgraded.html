Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA)

Transforms high-dimensional data by calculating D-dimensional mean vectors to compute the Between-class scatter matrix and the Within-class scatter matrix, extracting eigenvectors to maximize class separation. [Paper 5, Pg. 2, Sec. II-B.4]

Locally Linear Embedding (LLE)A non-linear dimensionality reduction technique that preserves local distances within data clusters.

Computes neighborhood preserving embeddings by mapping a D×N matrix to a d×N matrix using Euclidean distance to reconstruct data points from their K-nearest neighbors. [Paper 5, Pg. 2, Sec. II-B.1]

Cluster Ensembles (LinkCluE)A framework that combines multiple base clustering algorithms to form a stronger, unified classification boundary.

Relies on three link-based similarity matrices: Connected-Triple-based Similarity (CTS), SimRank-based SimilarityA graph-theoretic measure saying two nodes are similar if they are referenced by similar nodes. (SRS), and approximate SimRank-based Similarity (ASRS). [Paper 5, Pg. 3, Sec. II-C.3]

Feature Extraction & Active E-Noses

Active E-Noses & Temp Modulation

The rationale behind temperature modulation is that one sensor operating at multiple heating voltages produces multiple distinct patterns, significantly lowering the required sensor array cost.

"Active" E-noses are an evolution of these systems, employing an adaptive optimization of operating temperatures, recognizing that not all heating voltages contribute positively to classification.

Feature Extraction Pipeline

Feature extraction is the first and most critical step of recognition, comprising three main stages:

  • 1. Normalization

    Removes scaling effects caused by odorant concentration. Formula:

    $$ x_{ij} = \frac{x_{ij}}{\max(x_i)}, \quad i = 1,...,d; \quad j = 1,...,n $$
  • 2. Feature Selection (PCA / LDA)

    Identifies the most informative and discriminative subsets. Extracted features $\hat{X}$ based on linear transformation matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$:

    $$ \hat{X} = W^T X $$
  • 3. Feature Enhancement & Representation

    Targets best features in other domains (e.g., Fourier transform, dynamic social impact theory). Also involves dictionary algorithms to minimize reconstruction error.

1. Discreteness

Issue: Sensor reproducibility and batch variability.

Solution: A global affine transformation method is introduced for E-nose instruments batch calibration.

2. Drift

Issue: Systematical changes in sensor baseline over time.

Solution: An unsupervised feature adaptation model is proposed to achieve effective drift adaptation.

Denoising Algorithms

Denoising algorithms pursue noise removal on data mixed with unknown noise. These techniques operate across preprocessing and feature extraction stages.

1. Smooth Filter

A window moving average filter applied in real-time sensing. It mitigates extreme spikes by dropping the max and min values within window $q$.

$$ \hat{S}(j) = \frac{\sum_{i=j}^{j+q-1} S(i) - \max\{S(j),...,S(j+q-1)\} - \min\{S(j),...,S(j+q-1)\}}{q-2} $$

2. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

Noise removal achieved by truncating components to the $k$ largest singular values and reconstructing the noiseless data.

$$ \hat{\mathbf{X}} = \sum_{i=1}^k \hat{\sigma}_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T $$

3. Independent Component Analysis (ICA)

Higher-order statistical method for non-Gaussian data. Solves a linear un-mixing system $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ to estimate source signals $S$.

$$ X = AS \implies \hat{X} = WX = WAS \approx S $$

Drift Compensation Algorithms

Sensor drift caused by unknown dynamic processes (aging, poisoning, etc.) seriously deteriorates classification. Since drifted data cannot fit the original training data probability distribution, online adaptation and compensation are strictly required.

Component Correction based PCA (CCPCA)

Finds the drift direction $\mathbf{p}$ and explicitly corrects the data.

$$ \widehat{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{X} - (\mathbf{X}p)\mathbf{p}^T $$

Orthogonal Signal Correction (OSC)

Removes drift components orthogonal to the data.

$$ \widehat{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{X} - \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{t}_i \mathbf{p}_i^T $$

Domain Adaptation Extreme Learning Machines (DAELM)

A powerful transfer learning model for drift adaptation. It balances the learned classifier $\boldsymbol{\beta}_S$ across source (clean) and target (drifted) domains.

$$ \min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_S, \boldsymbol{\xi}_S^i, \boldsymbol{\xi}_T^j} \frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_S\|^2 + C_S \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N_S} \|\boldsymbol{\xi}_S^i\|^2 + C_T \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{N_T} \|\boldsymbol{\xi}_T^j\|^2 $$
$$ \text{s.t.} \begin{cases} \mathbf{H}_S^i \boldsymbol{\beta}_S = \mathbf{t}_S^i - \boldsymbol{\xi}_S^i, & i=1,...,N_S \\ \mathbf{H}_T^j \boldsymbol{\beta}_S = \mathbf{t}_T^j - \boldsymbol{\xi}_T^j, & j=1,...,N_T \end{cases} $$

Where $\mathbf{H}_S$ and $\mathbf{H}_T$ denote hand-crafted data matrices for clean and drifted data, $\mathbf{t}_S$ and $\mathbf{t}_T$ are labels, and $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ represents prediction errors.

Additive Factor Correction (EA)

Optimizes the correction matrix using an Evolutionary Algorithm.

$$ X_{corrected} = X + XM $$

Industrialization Challenge

Commercialized E-noses still face immense challenges with discreteness (reproducibility), drift recovery, and nontarget disturbance counteraction. Solving these is critical for large-scale industrial deployment.

Unsupervised Feature Adaptation (UFA)

Minimizes distribution discrepancy in principal component (PC) subspace between clean ($X_S$) and drifted ($X_T$) data without requiring labels.

$$ \min_W \|P_S - P_T W\|_F^2 + \lambda\|W\|_F^2 $$
$$ W^* = \frac{1}{\lambda + 1} P_T^T P_S $$

Where $P_S$ and $P_T$ are the subspace projections of the source and target domains obtained via PCA, and $W$ is the subspace alignment matrix.

Deep Dive: Global Affine Transformation (GAT) for Discreteness

Baseline Difference: The sensitive resistance $R_o$ of identical sensors in clear air differs, resulting in different output voltages.

Sensitivity Difference: Identical sensors have different sensitivity $R_s/R_o$ to the same odorant.

To address this sensor-specific affine variance, the GAT model enhances reproducibility:

$$ y_k = a_k x_k + b_k, \quad k = 1,...,m $$

Where $x_i$ is the slave instrument response, $y_i$ is the master instrument response, and $a_i, b_i$ are calibration parameters solved via Euclidean distance sample selection.

3D Solutions: GAT, UFA, & T3SRC Algorithms

Meticulous breakdown of the sample selection, subspace adaptation, and disturbance rejection algorithms.

GAT Euclidean Sample Selection

To solve the GAT coefficients with minimal labor cost, optimal samples are selected:

  1. Calculate pairwise Euclidean distance in master training set; select the farthest two patterns $s_1, s_2$. Set $S = \{s_1, s_2\}$.
  2. For each sample $x_i$, compute distance to set $S$ and log nearest distance $d_i$.
  3. Select sample with farthest distance in $d$ as $s_3$. Update $S$.
  4. Repeat until 5 samples are selected for calibration transfer.

Algorithm 1: UFA & MDD

  • Step 1: Solve $P_S$ by using PCA on $X_S$;
  • Step 2: Solve $P_T$ by using PCA on $X_T$;
  • Step 3: Obtain $W^*$ by solving alignment model;
  • Step 4: Calculate $X_{T}^{new} = P_T W^* X_T$;
  • Step 5: Compute $X_{S}^{new} = P_S^T X_S$;

To quantify the distribution difference between domains, we use Mean Distribution Discrepancy (MDD):

$$ \text{MDD}(\Theta_1, \Theta_2) = \left\| \frac{1}{N_1} \sum_{i=1}^{N_1} \theta_{1(i)} - \frac{1}{N_2} \sum_{j=1}^{N_2} \theta_{2(j)} \right\|_2 $$

Targets-to-Targets Self-Representation Classifier (T3SRC)

Nontarget disturbances in the real world are vast. T3SRC uses dictionary learning to memorize target gases via a representation matrix $\alpha$. The model rejects unknown disturbances by identifying large representation errors.

Total Representation Error (TRE) Model:

$$ X = X\alpha + E $$

Regularized Objective Function:

$$ \min_{\alpha} \|X - X\alpha\|_F^2 + \eta\|\alpha\|_2^2 $$

Closed-form Solution:

$$ \alpha = (X^T X + \eta I)^{-1} X^T X $$

Sensor Hardware & Pinout Schematics

Wiring, architecture, and calibration requirements for the sensor array.

Introduction & Motivation

Software is only as good as the hardware generating its signals. This section documents the physical layer: the microcontrollers (Arduino Mega, ESP), the ADC pin routing, and the rigorous physical calibration burn-in periods necessary to prevent floating values and sensor drift.

A. Integral Components of Our Edge Olfaction Node

Building on the analogy of human olfaction, our system mirrors biology by uniting a sensor array, sampling unit, and signal conditioning circuit. The sampling unit passes odor molecules into our array, which utilizes four distinct Metal Oxide Semiconductor (MOS) gas sensors: MQ-135, MQ-3, MQ-4, and MQ-2.

MOS sensors detect changes in electrical conductivity when exposed to VOCs emitted by food, enabling them to monitor spoilage in meats, poultry, and seafood [63], [64]. Additionally, they are adept at assessing the freshness of fruits and vegetables since they provide high sensitivity, low power consumption, and rapid response times for detecting a wide range of gases in real-time applications [7]. Despite their advantageous features, MOS sensors may face challenges such as cross-sensitivity and the necessity for elevated operating temperatures.

The signal conditioning circuit amplifies these analog responses. While commercial systems often use standalone DAC/ADC units, our Edge Node replaces these with an integrated Arduino-based microcontroller. By relying on the Arduino's built-in ADCs, we drastically reduce system complexity and power consumption.

Multimodal Extensions

We extend beyond traditional E-Nose literature by incorporating physical and environmental disturbance tracking directly into the data packet:

  • Dual DHT11 Setup: One tracks external ambient conditions, while the second tracks internal chamber conditions to accurately compensate for SnO2 lattice drift.
  • Piezo Vibration (Pin A4): Continuously sampled via an op-amp to detect physical handling or drops, recording the peak force during each 1.5s window.
  • PIR & Ultrasonic: Provides immediate proximity alerts and security checks at the edge node.

B. Selection of Sensors

The selection of sensors for specific food items depends on the odors associated with its quality or safety. We chose an array that guarantees overlapping coverage of key spoilage indicators (Ammonia via MQ-135, Alcohols via MQ-3, Methane via MQ-4, and Combustibles via MQ-2).

Optimizing Sensor Arrays: To achieve optimal performance, it is essential that the selected sensors provide complementary information while minimizing computational overhead. While literature often uses submodular optimization or genetic algorithms to address Feature Selection Algorithm (FSA) instability, our architecture relies on robust algorithmic smoothing (DWT/EMA) and specific sensor pairing to ensure consistent, stable data generation.

A primary concern is the standardization of sensor data and the development of robust algorithms for multimodal data fusion. Given the diversity of sensor types and manufacturers, achieving consistency and accuracy requires comprehensive calibration protocols and rigorous validation across different food categories and environmental conditions. Furthermore, the integration of heterogeneous data from E-nose and E-eye systems necessitates advanced ML and DL models capable of handling diverse input formats and generating reliable, interpretable predictions.

Hardware Integration & Chamber Specifications

Chamber Construction: The sampling unit was constructed from an airtight plastic food container to isolate emitted VOCs. The sensor array was mounted on the chamber lid via a 10mm drilled hole, rigorously insulated with silicone sealant to prevent ambient contamination.

Topography & Grounding: The architecture was centralized around an Arduino Mega 2560. All electrical components, including the custom analog breakout board, were ensured to have parallel grounding. Control peripherals included 3 LEDs and a 3-way toggle switch configured with pull-down resistors to guarantee zero floating values during toggles.

Burn-In & Purging Protocols: Calibration necessitated a 48-hour continuous preheating burn-in for virgin MQ sensors, and an extensive 15-60 minute stabilization preheat (depending on the target) before subsequent sessions. To prevent cross-contamination between biological samples (e.g., overlapping Cavendish banana decay signatures), the chamber was actively purged using a hairdryer for 5 minutes post-extraction.

Baseline Correction & Math: Before every test, zero air was injected into the chamber under rigidly controlled environmental baselines (e.g., 25±1°C, 55±2% humidity) to establish the initial baseline resistance (R₀). The true intensity of the sensor's reaction to VOCs was then normalized using the percentage change equation: Sensing Response = [(R₀ - R) / R₀] × 100, where R is the resistance after exposure.

  • Imaging Sensors: RGB cameras, CCD, CMOS, NIR/FTIR, UV imaging, and hyperspectral cameras. [Pg. 10, Sec. IV-B]
  • Color Space Transformations: Converts standard RGB inputs to HSV and CIELAB to separate luminance from chromaticity for accurate discoloration tracking. [Pg. 11, Sec. IV-C]
  • Segmentation & Extraction: Applies thresholding, region-growing, and edge detection to isolate the fruit. Extracts features using Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) and Local Binary Patterns (LBP). [Pg. 11, Sec. IV-C]
  • DL Architectures: Employs VGG, ResNet50, and YOLO versions. Mid-level feature fusion (combining E-Nose VOC features with E-Eye visual descriptors before classification) achieved a 98.6% classification accuracy. [Pg. 12, Sec. V-B]
Deep Tech Dive

Chemical Interference & Noise Filtration

Strategies for mitigating overlapping gas responses and humidity drift.

Introduction & Motivation

The biggest flaw of MOS sensors is their lack of selectivity; an ammonia sensor might also trigger in the presence of alcohol. Furthermore, ambient humidity physically alters the SnO2 lattice. This section documents the signal-to-noise limitations of raw hardware and the exact calibration strategies (DWT, drift compensation) required to prevent false positives.

Humidity & SnO₂ Lattice Physics

Ambient water vapor ($H_2O$) mechanically displaces adsorbed oxygen ions ($O^-$ and $O^{2-}$) on the heated tin dioxide ($SnO_2$) crystalline lattice surface of MOX sensors. Because these sensors are fundamentally "reducing gas" detectors, this displacement causes electrons to be released back into the conduction band. This lowers the sensor's overall resistance—mimicking the exact electrical signature of a VOC gas exposure. Therefore, a sudden spike in humidity looks electrically identical to a sudden spike in ethanol, necessitating the rigorous DHT11/BME280 compensation math found in our `calibration_logger.py`.

Edge Latency & Compute Specs

Processing times for executing neural networks on constrained edge hardware.

Introduction & Motivation

Pushing heavy Deep Learning models to the cloud introduces network latency and privacy risks. The future of food inspection lies in "Edge Intelligence." The motivation here is to baseline the exact inference speeds—measured in milliseconds—for running complex PCA, SVM, and CNN architectures directly on edge nodes like Raspberry Pis or ESP microcontrollers.

Real-Time Processing Constraints

Edge computing vastly improves system responsiveness by processing data at the node rather than the cloud. However, running complex algorithms like Discrete Wavelet Transforms (DWT) or K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) across a 128-feature dimensional space induces heavy CPU blocking and latency spikes on constrained hardware (like the Arduino Mega or Raspberry Pi).

SOLUTION LOGIC:

Main Loop -> Runs fast EMA (Exponential Moving Avg) filter exclusively.

Thread Pool -> Asynchronous worker runs DWT & Robust Standardization every 10s.

Result -> 0.0ms main loop blocking; Real-time responsiveness preserved.

Error & Confusion Matrix Analysis

Where the models failed and why.

Introduction & Motivation

A model boasting 100% accuracy is rarely flawless in the real world. By analyzing confusion matrices from the literature, we can understand the edge cases—such as a heavily bruised banana being chemically misclassified as "overripe" rather than "ripe" because its VOC emission outpaced its visual coloring.

When the Math Disagrees with the Eye

A specific failure mode was analyzed in the Banana Ripeness (Paper 4) dataset regarding "Sample B2". Subjectively and temporally, B2 was logged as a "ripe" sample. However, the KNN model rigidly classified it as "overripe".

Upon analyzing the raw sensor matrix, B2 exhibited dramatically higher levels of MQ-135 (VOCs) and MQ-136 (Sulfur) compared to its peers. Physically, B2 possessed a higher concentration of necrotic black spots and a stronger off-odor. This "error" is actually a triumph of the E-Nose: it correctly identified the chemical reality of advanced decay, overriding the human visual schedule. This proves that chemical sensing often leads visual symptoms in spoilage detection.

Our system leverages a low-cost MQ-series array paired with an Arduino Mega. Pathogen & Spoilage Profiling

Granular datasets on fungal and bacterial detection.

Introduction & Motivation

Visual inspection is useless when the rot occurs inside the core of a fruit or beneath the soil. The motivation for this section is to highlight the E-nose's ability to act as a non-invasive diagnostic tool, identifying the unique metabolic off-gassing signatures of specific destructive pathogens like Erwinia carotovora or Fusarium.

Microbial Metabolic Signatures

The papers detailed specific detection matrices for highly destructive agricultural pathogens, most notably Fusarium (fungal rot in onions) and Pectobacterium carotovorum / Erwinia (bacterial soft rot in potatoes).

Chemical Mechanics: These pathogens rapidly breakdown complex plant carbohydrates and proteins into specific Volatile Sulfur Compounds (VSCs), ammonia, and long-chain aldehydes during advanced necrotic stages. The E-Nose array detects the surge of these unique metabolic byproducts long before external fungal mycelium or bacterial slime is visible to the human eye, enabling preventative culling in supply chains.
