A combination of sub-threshold toxins in a closed room can cause severe respiratory distress and cognitive impairment. The HRI dynamically sums these weighted fractions ($C_i/TLV_i$) and triggers an alert for the *combined* environmental risk before it becomes dangerous!

A. Detection & Environmental Interferences

The sensing mechanism of the MQ-4 relies on baseline resistance ($R_0$) and gas-sensitive resistance ($R_S$), both of which are highly susceptible to drift under varying ambient conditions.

Variation in temperature can change the kinetics of gas adsorption and desorption on the surface. Similarly, humidity can interfere with the conduction paths. Therefore, correcting these environmental interferences is essential for achieving accurate, real-time methane values in open environments for GIS-based mapping.

B. Mathematical Quantification & Calibration

The calibration curve is derived from the datasheet log-log relationship between the concentration ($x$) and the resistance ratio.

1. Voltage Divider Equation

$$ R_S = R_L \left(\frac{V_H}{V_{RL}} - 1\right) $$

Where $V_H=5V$ and $R_L=10k\Omega$.

2. Log-Log Linearization

$$ \log\left(\frac{R_S}{R_0}\right) = B \cdot \log(x) + \log(A) $$

3. Exact Quantification Formula

Using boundary points $200$ ppm ($1.8$) and $10,000$ ppm ($0.45$), we solve for constants $A=11.77$ and $B=-0.354$, resulting in the concentration ($x$) equation:

$$ x = 1058.75 \left(\frac{R_S}{R_0}\right)^{-2.825} $$

💡 Lucid Understanding: Why do we use Log-Log curves for sensors?
The Principle: Chemical resistance does not change in a straight, linear line. If the gas concentration in the air doubles, the resistance doesn't halve; it follows an exponential power law.

By taking the logarithm of both the resistance ratio (Y-axis) and the gas concentration (X-axis), the curved exponential line flattens out into a perfectly straight line ($y = mx + c$). Once the line is straight, we can use basic high school algebra to mathematically derive the exact constants ($A$ and $B$) and accurately predict PPM for any resistance reading!

Temperature & Humidity Drift Correction

To guarantee absolute accuracy across diverse field conditions, thermodynamic correction factors for temperature ($C_T$) and relative humidity ($C_H$) are applied to the final model (where $T_{cal} = 25^\circ C$ and $RH_{cal} = 60\%$):

Temperature Correction

$$ C_T = e^{\frac{T - T_{cal}}{20}} $$

Humidity Correction

$$ C_H = 1 + 0.02(RH - RH_{cal}) $$

Final Environmentally Corrected Gas Equation:

$$ x = 1058.75 \left(\frac{R_S}{R_0}\right)^{-2.825} \cdot C_T \cdot C_H $$

The Chemiresistive Principle

A suite of MQ series gas sensors (MQ2, MQ4, MQ8, and MQ9) enables the system to identify various hazardous gases. These sensors operate based on the chemiresistive principle, where the electrical resistance of the sensing element changes in the presence of target gases.

The sensing material, typically $SnO_2$ (tin dioxide), interacts with gas molecules, altering the surface conductivity. When exposed to reducing gases like methane ($CH_4$), LPG, CO, or hydrogen ($H_2$), the resistance decreases due to electron donation to the conduction band.

The gas concentration $C$ is derived using the empirical resistance ratio:

$$ \frac{R_s}{R_0} = A \cdot C^B $$

Where $R_s$ is resistance in gas, $R_0$ is clean air baseline, and $A, B$ are constants.

IoT Closed-Loop Freshness Preservation

Integrating real-time sensing, active actuation, and cloud-based ML for perishable food management.

The Problem: Passive Monitoring

Preserving the freshness of perishable produce (like climacteric fruits) is a global challenge. Bananas ripen quickly and give off ethylene and carbon dioxide, which speeds up spoilage [1].

Early studies used high-accuracy e-nose sensors to detect these spoilage gases [9][14], but they were limited by high cost and laboratory-only feasibility. Furthermore, most existing systems either focus on passive monitoring without feedback, or predictive modeling without real-time control, leaving the actual environment unchanged and leading to significant post-harvest losses.

The Solution: Closed-Loop Control

This study introduces a low-cost, IoT-enabled framework that combines real-time sensing with active environmental control. It uses a DHT11 sensor (temperature/humidity), an MQ-135 sensor (ethylene and CO₂ via Rs/Ro calibration), and an ESP32 controller.

Crucially, the system operates a Peltier cooler and ventilation fan to actively regulate the environment. Sensor data is sent to Firebase for visualization, while a cloud-based Machine Learning model predicts freshness in real time [7][13].

💡 Lucid Understanding: Passive vs Closed-Loop Control
The Principle: 90% of "Smart IoT" projects on the internet are passive. They read a sensor and display a graph on a phone app. But if your bananas are rotting and your system just sends you an alert while you are asleep, the bananas still rot!

Closed-Loop Control means the system takes physical action by itself. When the MQ-135 detects high Ethylene, the ESP32 physically clicks a relay to turn on an exhaust fan. When the DHT11 detects high heat, it turns on a Peltier cooler. The loop is closed: Sensor $\rightarrow$ Brain $\rightarrow$ Actuator $\rightarrow$ Environment Changes $\rightarrow$ Sensor. This transforms a basic monitoring tool into a fully autonomous preservation machine.

The Calibration Problem

Calibration is the critical process of obtaining the most accurate sensor data. MQ gas sensors are designed to measure different types of gas simultaneously, but the standard datasheet formulas for calibration are notoriously poor and inaccurate in real-world scenarios.

To solve this problem, two fundamental steps are required:
1) Know exactly what the measured substance is.
2) Identify the specific formula for that environment.

This article presents a robust method of calibrating gas sensors using LabView. The acquisition system consists of a sensor network that transmits raw data to an acquisition board, and ultimately to the computer.

Used to mathematically identify the calibration equation that maps raw sensor data to true concentration, bypassing the flawed datasheet defaults.

This perfectly validates our entire approach of abandoning the standard MQ datasheets! Since the datasheet formulas are scientifically inadequate, our custom Python backend using machine learning (like ResGAT and Linear Regression) to derive our own custom calibration curves on the Raspberry Pi is the only correct path forward for enterprise-grade accuracy.

Data Acquisition & Nonlinear Regression

The reason to study the calibration of gas sensors is the error occurred after the data acquisition. Usually the calibration formula is implemented on the acquisition board. In this project, we implement the gas sensors calibration using LabView interface. The gases values data are collected with an Arduino Mega 2560 board. To receive the data on the computer using LabView 2013, we tested two methods of data acquisition (LIFA Base soft and VISA card virtual instrument).

MQ sensors are most used in applications due to their low price and reasonably good lifespan and sensitivity characteristics. It is commercially available in a wide variety of models. In the calibration process, we use a voltage sensor to adapt a voltage result of gas sensor.

The first formula shows that the MOS sensor is nonlinear with gas concentration:

$$ \frac{R}{R_0} = (1 + k_{gas} C_{gas})^{-\beta} \quad (1) $$

Where $R$ is sensor resistance, $R_0$ is resistance in air, $C_{gas}$ is gas concentration, $\beta$ is characteristic power, $k_{gas}$ depends on gas.

The formula is a power function with a negative exponent ($y = ax^b, b < 0$). We solve the nonlinear regression equation using the formulas:

$$ ppm = a \left( \frac{R_s}{R_0} \right)^b \quad (3) $$ $$ \log(ppm) = \log(a) + b \log\left( \frac{R_s}{R_0} \right) \quad (4) $$ $$ ppm = 10^{\log(a) + b \log(R_s/R_0)} \quad (5) $$
💡 Lucid Understanding: Why Logarithms?
The Principle: The relationship between the gas concentration ($ppm$) and the sensor's resistance ($R_s/R_0$) is a curved line (a power function). Computers (and algorithms like Linear Regression) hate curves because they are hard to predict.

By applying a logarithm to both sides of the equation (Equation 4), we magically flatten that curve into a perfectly straight line! Once it is a straight line, it becomes incredibly easy to find the slope ($b$) and the starting point ($\log a$). Equation 5 is just reversing the logarithm (using $10^x$) to get the final PPM value back.

For example, we use the MQ2 sensor to extract the points on the graph ($H_2 ppm = f(R_s/R_0)$) for $H_2$. A set of points are extracted using the WebPlotDigitizer to get a mathematical model that matches the data.

The Principle: Equation 11 is exactly what we write in Python to translate the raw ADC voltage coming into the Raspberry Pi into a physical resistance value.

Notice how this is mathematically identical to the ResGAT voltage divider equation ($R_s = R_L(\frac{V_{cc}}{V_{out}} - 1)$) we analyzed earlier. They just rearranged the algebra! $\frac{V_c - V_{out}}{V_{out}}$ is mathematically exactly the same as $\frac{V_c}{V_{out}} - \frac{V_{out}}{V_{out}}$, which equals $\frac{V_c}{V_{out}} - 1$. This proves that the physics model holds perfectly true across completely different research papers!

Deriving the Baseline Resistance ($R_0$)

Calibration is a process to obtain the most accurate sensor data. One of the problems is that each of these sensors is produced to measure different types of gas at the same time. If a sample emitted two or more of the gases the sensor can measure, the results will be inconclusive. To solve this problem, one must know the measured substance.

The charts provided by the manufacturer are made with a resistive value $R_L$ (load resistance). Knowing $V_c$, $R_L$, and $V_{out}$, we first compute the sensor resistance in Clean Air ($R_{sCA}$) using formula (12):

$$ R_{sCA} = R_L \frac{V_c - V_{out}}{V_{out}} \quad (12) $$

After that, the baseline resistance $R_0$ is computed with formula (13), where $RatioAir$ is a constant deduced from the datasheet (14):

$$ \frac{R_{sCA}}{R_0} = f(ppm_{CA}) = RatioAir \quad (14) $$ $$ R_0 = \frac{R_{sCA}}{RatioAir} \quad (13) $$

After computing $R_0$, the calibration process is finished.

💡 Lucid Understanding: What exactly is $R_0$?
The Principle: $R_0$ is the sensor's "baseline" resistance in perfectly clean, uncontaminated air.

You can't measure how much gas is in the room if you don't know what "zero gas" looks like! The manufacturer's datasheet gives us a constant called RatioAir (which is just the expected ratio of $R_s / R_0$ in clean air). By measuring the sensor's actual resistance in a clean room ($R_{sCA}$) and dividing it by that constant, we mathematically lock in our baseline $R_0$. From then on, all gas measurements are calculated relative to that baseline.

💡 Lucid Understanding: Why does a vacuum trigger the sensor?
The Principle: This perfectly proves the Wolkenstein Adsorption Model we discussed in the ResGAT paper!

Remember that in clean air, Oxygen ($O_2$) molecules trap electrons on the sensor surface, creating high resistance. When a target gas hits it, it reacts with the Oxygen, releasing the electrons and dropping the resistance.

But what happens if you just suck all the air out of the room (a vacuum)? There is no Oxygen left to trap the electrons! The resistance plummets drastically, making the sensor output voltage spike as if there is a massive gas leak. This physically proves that MQ sensors don't technically "detect gas"—they detect the absence of oxygen on their surface!

The Physics of Drift: Wolkenstein Adsorption Model

The Wolkenstein adsorption model describes chemisorption on semiconductor surfaces by linking surface adsorption to electronic structure and band bending. The sensing mechanism of an n-type semiconductor like Tin Dioxide ($SnO_2$) involves the chemi-sorption of oxygen species ($O^-$, $O_2^-$) on the grain boundaries. In clean air, these oxygen species trap electrons from the conduction band, thus creating a high-potential barrier between grains and consequently high electrical resistance.

In the presence of a reducing gas (e.g., CO), the gas molecules react with the surface oxygen, releasing the trapped electrons back into the conduction band. This lowers the potential barrier and decreases the resistance $R_s$. This relationship is highly non-linear and is governed by the empirical power law derived from surface adsorption kinetics, as defined below:

$$R_s = A \cdot [C]^{-\alpha} \cdot \Theta(T, H)$$

  • $R_s$ = Sensor Resistance
  • $[C]$ = Gas Concentration (PPM)
  • $\alpha$ = Gas Sensitivity Slope
  • $\Theta(T, H)$ = Temperature/Humidity Modulation

Hardware Bridge (Voltage Divider Inversion): Standard AI models fail because they try to map the raw ADC voltage ($V_{out}$) directly to the Gas Concentration ($[C]$), forcing the neural network to guess a highly complex, non-linear chemical curve.

Instead, because our MQ sensors on the Edge Olfaction device are physically wired in a voltage divider circuit alongside a load resistor ($R_L$), we must explicitly calculate the true sensor resistance ($R_s$) using standard MOS circuit physics before we pass the data into the ResGAT model:

$$R_s = R_L \left( \frac{V_{cc}}{V_{out}} - 1 \right)$$

The ResGAT Topology

ResGAT solves this by fundamentally changing the math before it even hits the neural network. First, it applies a Logarithmic Transformation to the power law:

$$\log(R_s) \propto -\alpha \log([C]) + \log(\Theta(T, H))$$
  • This transformation turns a complex non-linear curve into a simpler linear addition, accelerating AI training.
  • The Graph Attention Network then physically links the sensors via "Edges". It mathematically forces the AI to recognize that Temperature changes are causing the resistance drop, preventing false positives!

Graph Topology Construction

ResGAT defines the sensor array as a directed graph $G = (V, E)$. The nodes ($V$) represent the actual hardware sensors: $V = \{v_{MQ7}, v_{MQ135}, v_{Temp}, v_{Hum}\}$.

Environmental Edges ($E_{env}$)

Directed Edges: $(v_{Temp} \rightarrow v_{MQ7})$. These force the AI to learn the thermal drift modulation factor $\Theta(T, H)$. If Temp goes up but gas stays constant, the model assigns heavy weight to this edge, recognizing the resistance drop as a weather change, not a gas leak.

Interference Edges ($E_{int}$)

Bi-directional Edges: $(v_{MQ7} \leftrightarrow v_{MQ135})$. These teach the AI Cross-Sensitivity. If both MQ-7 and MQ-135 drop simultaneously, the graph infers a wide-spectrum interferent (like Alcohol). If only MQ-7 drops, it isolates it as pure CO.

Dynamic Attention Coefficients

Unlike older models (GCNs) that use static, rigid weights, the ResGAT uses a Multi-Layer Graph Attention Network (GATv2). It dynamically calculates an attention coefficient ($\alpha_{ij}$) representing the exact real-time importance of node $j$'s features to node $i$:

$$ \alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(\text{LeakyReLU}(\mathbf{a}^T [W\mathbf{h}_i || W\mathbf{h}_j]))}{\sum_{k \in \mathcal{N}_i} \exp(\text{LeakyReLU}(\mathbf{a}^T [W\mathbf{h}_i || W\mathbf{h}_k]))} $$

This mechanism actively filters out "Spurious Correlations" (false positives) by balancing the environmental noise against the actual chemical reactions.

Open Set Domain Adaptation (OSDA) for Sensor Drift

Handling unpredictable, open-environment sensor drift when training data and real-world data don't perfectly match.

The "Open Environment" Challenge

Most E-nose models assume a perfect lab setting where the training data has the exact same categories as the real-world drift data. However, in an open environment, unknown samples (like random background smells or contaminated air) blend in, distorting the data distribution and ruining the model's accuracy. Previous models (like Liu et al.) fail because they ignore situations where the real-world data has more unknown categories than the training data.

Three Major Open-Set Challenges:
  • Unknown category samples appear in both the training phase and real-world drift phase.
  • There are no prior labels for the drift and these unknown background smells.
  • The unknown categories differ entirely between the training and drift phases.

The OSDA Paradigm Solution

To solve this, the study introduces an OSDA (Open Set Domain Adaptation) model. It divides all incoming sensor data into:

  • Known Categories: The target smells we actually want to recognize (e.g., specific spoilage VOCs).
  • Unknown Categories: The uncertain, unpredictable background noise samples.

Key Methodological Contributions

  • Covariance ConstraintA mathematical rule enforcing second-order alignment of data distributions to keep the target signals mathematically stable.: Used for second-order alignment.
  • Category LabelerAn algorithm module that actively estimates labels to separate the known targets from the unknown background noise.: Introduced specifically for unknown category separation.
  • This is the first OSDA model designed for all-around, open-set drift compensation.

To maintain IEEE-aligned accuracy, we may need to incorporate an OSDA Category Labeler algorithm into our Python filtering pipeline to actively separate target spoilage VOCs from random warehouse odors over long periods.

Evolution of Domain Adaptation (DA) to OSDA

Traditional Domain Adaptation (DA) techniques (like TCA, JDA, and DDC) try to transfer learned knowledge from a clean "source" domain to a messy "target" domain. However, these older methods demand that both domains have identical categories. In open-set environments, where unknown variables interfere, these models collapse. Thus, OSDA was born to treat known categories as "public" data and unknown categories as "private" data.

The Two Roads of OSDA Methodologies:

1. Feature Space Projection

These methods focus on projecting data into new mathematical spaces to align target domains.

  • JACS: Uses Maximum Mean Difference (MMD) to guide category projections.
  • Hyperspherical Alignment: Projects features into a spherical latent space for centroid alignment.
  • Adversarial Learning: Uses adversarial networks to align and separate the knowns and unknowns simultaneously.

2. Sample Weighting

These methods focus on evaluating each individual data sample to estimate if it belongs to an unknown category.

  • Iterative Updating: Computes and dynamically updates the weight of each sample based on its transferability.
  • Positive-Unlabeled Methods: Reweights source/target data to isolate novel, interfering classes.
The Remaining Gap:

Despite these advancements, older OSDA methods strictly restrict where unknown categories can appear (e.g., they must exist only in the source, or identically in both). For real-world sensor drift, a new model is needed that allows the "free appearance" of unknown categories anywhere.

Proposed Methodology: Mathematical Notations

Before defining the algorithm, the paper establishes a rigid mathematical framework to describe how data is structured across the clean training data (Source) and the real-world drift data (Target).

Source Domain (Training Data)

  • $D_S$: Number of sensor features.
  • $N_S$: Total number of training samples.
  • $X_S \in \mathbb{R}^{D_S \times N_S}$: The total training data matrix.
  • The data is split into Known ($X_S^k$) and Unknown ($X_S^u$) samples.
  • Labels ($Y_S$): A binary vector of size $(C+1)$, where $C$ is the number of target odors, and the $+1$ represents the "unknown" bucket.

Target Domain (Drift Data)

  • $D_T$: Number of sensor features in the real world.
  • $N_T$: Total number of live samples collected.
  • $X_T \in \mathbb{R}^{D_T \times N_T}$: The live data matrix.
  • Live data is also split into Known ($\hat{N}_T^k$) and Unknown ($\hat{N}_T^u$).
  • Pseudo-Labels ($\hat{Y}_T$): Because we don't know the real answers in the live environment, the system assigns estimated "pseudo-labels".
Deep Technical Dive: Matrix Expansion

The total source matrix is a concatenation of the known and unknown sets:

$$ X_S = [X_S^k, X_S^u] $$

Where the known matrix $X_S^k$ contains vectors $x_{s,1}^k, ..., x_{s,N_S^k}^k$ and the unknown matrix $X_S^u$ contains vectors $x_{s,1}^u, ..., x_{s,N_S^u}^u$.

Model Formulation (MKDUS)

The paper proposes the MKDUS model (Model for Known sample drift elimination and unknown sample separation). It operates using three core components to clean up messy sensor data:

1. First-Order Alignment

Aligns the raw averages (means) of the sensor data between the lab and the real world.

2. Second-Order Alignment

Aligns the variance (spread/noise) of the data using covariance matrices.

3. Category Labeler

Assigns "pseudo-labels" to incoming live data to weed out the unknown background noise.

The Math: Known Category Alignment

To force the real-world data to match the clean lab data, the algorithm minimizes three distinct "Loss" functions. Matrices $A$ and $B$ are the mathematical filters applied to the Source and Target data respectively.

Marginal Distribution Loss ($\ell_{MG}$)Measures the total distance between the overall average of all known lab samples and all known live samples.

$$ \ell_{MG} = || A^T \bar{x}_s^k - B^T \bar{x}_t^k ||_2^2 $$

(Minimizes the gap between the overall means)

Conditional Distribution Loss ($\ell_{CD}$)Drills down deeper to measure the distance between specific target classes (e.g., comparing Lab 'Ripe' vs Live 'Ripe').

$$ \ell_{CD} = \sum_{n=1}^{N_C} || A^T \bar{x}_{ks}^{cn} - B^T \bar{x}_{kt}^{cn} ||_2^2 $$

(Minimizes the intra-category gap for each specific class)

Covariance Loss ($\ell_{Val}$)A second-order statistic ensuring that the variance (spread) of the data remains identical across both environments.

Instead of calculating a massive loss function, this is solved by mapping the projection matrices directly:

$$ A = C B \quad \text{where} \quad C = C_S^{-1/2} C_T^{-1/2} $$
Total Alignment Objective:
$$ \min_{A,B} \ell_c = \ell_{MG} + \ell_{CD} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad A = CB $$

Denoising Algorithms

Denoising algorithms pursue noise removal on data mixed with unknown noise. These techniques operate across preprocessing and feature extraction stages.

1. Smooth Filter

A window moving average filter applied in real-time sensing. It mitigates extreme spikes by dropping the max and min values within window $q$.

$$ \hat{S}(j) = \frac{\sum_{i=j}^{j+q-1} S(i) - \max\{S(j),...,S(j+q-1)\} - \min\{S(j),...,S(j+q-1)\}}{q-2} $$

2. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

Noise removal achieved by truncating components to the $k$ largest singular values and reconstructing the noiseless data.

$$ \hat{\mathbf{X}} = \sum_{i=1}^k \hat{\sigma}_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^T $$

3. Independent Component Analysis (ICA)

Higher-order statistical method for non-Gaussian data. Solves a linear un-mixing system $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ to estimate source signals $S$.

$$ X = AS \implies \hat{X} = WX = WAS \approx S $$

Drift Compensation Algorithms

Sensor drift caused by unknown dynamic processes (aging, poisoning, etc.) seriously deteriorates classification. Since drifted data cannot fit the original training data probability distribution, online adaptation and compensation are strictly required.

Component Correction based PCA (CCPCA)

Finds the drift direction $\mathbf{p}$ and explicitly corrects the data.

$$ \widehat{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{X} - (\mathbf{X}p)\mathbf{p}^T $$

Orthogonal Signal Correction (OSC)

Removes drift components orthogonal to the data.

$$ \widehat{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{X} - \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{t}_i \mathbf{p}_i^T $$

Domain Adaptation Extreme Learning Machines (DAELM)

A powerful transfer learning model for drift adaptation. It balances the learned classifier $\boldsymbol{\beta}_S$ across source (clean) and target (drifted) domains.

$$ \min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_S, \boldsymbol{\xi}_S^i, \boldsymbol{\xi}_T^j} \frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_S\|^2 + C_S \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N_S} \|\boldsymbol{\xi}_S^i\|^2 + C_T \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^{N_T} \|\boldsymbol{\xi}_T^j\|^2 $$
$$ \text{s.t.} \begin{cases} \mathbf{H}_S^i \boldsymbol{\beta}_S = \mathbf{t}_S^i - \boldsymbol{\xi}_S^i, & i=1,...,N_S \\ \mathbf{H}_T^j \boldsymbol{\beta}_S = \mathbf{t}_T^j - \boldsymbol{\xi}_T^j, & j=1,...,N_T \end{cases} $$

Where $\mathbf{H}_S$ and $\mathbf{H}_T$ denote hand-crafted data matrices for clean and drifted data, $\mathbf{t}_S$ and $\mathbf{t}_T$ are labels, and $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ represents prediction errors.

Additive Factor Correction (EA)

Optimizes the correction matrix using an Evolutionary Algorithm.

$$ X_{corrected} = X + XM $$

Industrialization Challenge

Commercialized E-noses still face immense challenges with discreteness (reproducibility), drift recovery, and nontarget disturbance counteraction. Solving these is critical for large-scale industrial deployment.

Unsupervised Feature Adaptation (UFA)

Minimizes distribution discrepancy in principal component (PC) subspace between clean ($X_S$) and drifted ($X_T$) data without requiring labels.

$$ \min_W \|P_S - P_T W\|_F^2 + \lambda\|W\|_F^2 $$
$$ W^* = \frac{1}{\lambda + 1} P_T^T P_S $$

Where $P_S$ and $P_T$ are the subspace projections of the source and target domains obtained via PCA, and $W$ is the subspace alignment matrix.

To address this sensor-specific affine variance, the GAT model enhances reproducibility:

Where $x_i$ is the slave instrument response, $y_i$ is the master instrument response, and $a_i, b_i$ are calibration parameters solved via Euclidean distance sample selection.

E-Nose Drift & Domain Adaptation

E-nose drift is a critical problem caused by gas sensor poisoning, aging, and varied environmental factors (e.g., temperature and humidity). It creates unpredictable sensing-response fluctuations, degrading recognition performance due to cumulative mismatch.

Traditional model-based methods fall into three categories:

  • Signal Processing: Abstracts drift-like components (PCA, ICA, OSC, DWT) but uses stationary criteria during signal decomposition.
  • Adaptive Classifier: Dynamically updates parameters (SOM, ART). However, they require expensive drift data labels.
  • Domain Adaptation (DA): Utilizes drift data distribution instead of labels. Examples include AELM (Adaptive Extreme Learning Machine) and DRCA (Domain Regularized Component Analysis).

Deep Technical Dive: Domain Alignment Loss Equations

1. Marginal Distribution Loss (\(\ell_{MG}\))

Calculates the known sample distance between source and target domains:

$$ \ell_{MG} = \left\| A^T \bar{x}_s^k - B^T \bar{x}_t^k \right\|_2^2 $$ $$ = \text{tr}\left( A^T \bar{x}_s^k (\bar{x}_s^k)^T A + B^T \bar{x}_t^k (\bar{x}_t^k)^T B - B^T \bar{x}_t^k (\bar{x}_s^k)^T A - A^T \bar{x}_s^k (\bar{x}_t^k)^T B \right) $$

Where \(\bar{x}_s^k\) and \(\bar{x}_t^k\) are the known part averages in the source and target domains. \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{D_S \times d}\) and \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{D_T \times d}\) are projection matrices.

2. Conditional Distribution Loss (\(\ell_{CD}\))

Equates the conditional distribution with the intra-category distance for each known category:

$$ \ell_{CD} = \sum_{n=1}^{N_C} \left\| A^T \bar{x}_{s}^{kn} - B^T \bar{x}_{t}^{kn} \right\|_2^2 $$

Where \(\bar{x}_{s}^{kn}\) and \(\bar{x}_{t}^{kn}\) represent the means of the nth known category in the source and target domains.

3. Variance Loss (\(\ell_{Val}\)) & Mapping Relationship

Evaluates domain alignment using second-order statistics to keep identical data variance across domains:

$$ \ell_{Val} = \left\| A^T C_S A - B^T C_T B \right\|_F^2 = 0 $$

This yields the mapping relationship:

$$ A = C B \quad \text{where} \quad C = C_S^{-1/2} C_T^{-1/2} $$

Where \(C_S = X_S^k (X_S^k)^T\) and \(C_T = X_T^k (X_T^k)^T\) represent the covariance matrices of known category samples.

Total Distribution Alignment Loss (\(\ell_c\))

Combining the marginal, conditional, and mapping constraints:

$$ \min_{A,B} \ell_c = \ell_{MG} + \ell_{CD} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad A = CB $$
Deep Technical Dive: Category Separation, Labeler & Model Solution

D. Known–Unknown Category Separation (\(\ell_u\))

Maximizes distance between known and unknown parts in the projected subspace:

$$ \max_{A,B} \ell_u = \ell_S^{k-u} + \ell_T^{k-u} $$ $$ = \text{tr}\left( A^T(\bar{x}_s^k - \bar{x}_s^u)(\bar{x}_s^k - \bar{x}_s^u)^T A + B^T(\bar{x}_t^k - \bar{x}_t^u)(\bar{x}_t^k - \bar{x}_t^u)^T B \right) $$

E. Total Loss Function (\(\ell\))

Couples distribution alignment (minimize) and category separation (maximize):

$$ \min_{A,B} \ell = \frac{\min_{A,B} \ell_c}{\ell_u} = \frac{\min_{A,B} \ell_{MG} + \ell_{CD}}{\ell_S^{k-u} + \ell_T^{k-u}} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad A = CB $$

F. Category Labeler

Uses Euclidean distance \(d_{i,n}\) to assign categories via 0-1 programming:

$$ d_{i,n} = \left\| B x_t^i - A \bar{x}_s^{ck_n} \right\|^2 $$ $$ \min_{\hat{y}_{t}^{i,n}, \hat{y}_{t}^{i,u}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_T} \left( \sum_{n=1}^{C} d_{i,n} \hat{y}_{t}^{i,n} + \lambda \hat{y}_{t}^{i,u} \right) $$

Subject to constraints ensuring every target sample is assigned exactly one label, and every known class has at least one target domain sample.

G. Model Solution via Eigenvalue Decomposition

Transforms the optimization by substituting mapping relationship and trace functions (\(D\) and \(E\)), adding orthogonality constraints:

$$ \min_B \frac{\text{tr}(B^T D B)}{\text{tr}(B^T E B)} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \text{tr}(B^T E B) = 1, \quad B^T B = I $$

Applying Lagrange multipliers yields the derivative matrix:

$$ (\gamma D - E) + (\gamma D - E)^T B^T = \phi B^T $$

Where \(B\) is solved by eigenvalue decomposition, and \(A\) is subsequently achieved via \(A = CB\).

GAT Euclidean Sample Selection

To solve the GAT coefficients with minimal labor cost, optimal samples are selected:

  1. Calculate pairwise Euclidean distance in master training set; select the farthest two patterns $s_1, s_2$. Set $S = \{s_1, s_2\}$.
  2. For each sample $x_i$, compute distance to set $S$ and log nearest distance $d_i$.
  3. Select sample with farthest distance in $d$ as $s_3$. Update $S$.
  4. Repeat until 5 samples are selected for calibration transfer.

A. Integral Components of Our Edge Olfaction Node

Building on the analogy of human olfaction, our system mirrors biology by uniting a sensor array, sampling unit, and signal conditioning circuit. MOS sensors detect changes in electrical conductivity when exposed to VOCs emitted by food, enabling them to monitor spoilage in meats, poultry, and seafood [63], [64]. Additionally, they are adept at assessing the freshness of fruits and vegetables since they provide high sensitivity, low power consumption, and rapid response times for detecting a wide range of gases in real-time applications [7]. Despite their advantageous features, MOS sensors may face challenges such as cross-sensitivity and the necessity for elevated operating temperatures.

The signal conditioning circuit amplifies these analog responses. While commercial systems often use standalone DAC/ADC units, our Edge Node replaces these with an integrated Arduino-based microcontroller. By relying on the Arduino's built-in ADCs, we drastically reduce system complexity and power consumption.

Hardware Integration & Chamber Specifications

Chamber Construction: The sampling unit was constructed from an airtight plastic food container to isolate emitted VOCs. The sensor array was mounted on the chamber lid via a 10mm drilled hole, rigorously insulated with silicone sealant to prevent ambient contamination.

Topography & Grounding: The architecture was centralized around an Arduino Mega 2560. All electrical components, including the custom analog breakout board, were ensured to have parallel grounding. Control peripherals included 3 LEDs and a 3-way toggle switch configured with pull-down resistors to guarantee zero floating values during toggles.

Burn-In & Purging Protocols: Calibration necessitated a 48-hour continuous preheating burn-in for virgin MQ sensors, and an extensive 15-60 minute stabilization preheat (depending on the target) before subsequent sessions. To prevent cross-contamination between biological samples (e.g., overlapping Cavendish banana decay signatures), the chamber was actively purged using a hairdryer for 5 minutes post-extraction.

Baseline Correction & Math: Before every test, zero air was injected into the chamber under rigidly controlled environmental baselines (e.g., 25±1°C, 55±2% humidity) to establish the initial baseline resistance (R₀). The true intensity of the sensor's reaction to VOCs was then normalized using the percentage change equation: Sensing Response = [(R₀ - R) / R₀] × 100, where R is the resistance after exposure.

The biggest flaw of MOS sensors is their lack of selectivity; an ammonia sensor might also trigger in the presence of alcohol. Furthermore, ambient humidity physically alters the SnO2 lattice. This section documents the signal-to-noise limitations of raw hardware and the exact calibration strategies (DWT, drift compensation) required to prevent false positives.

Humidity & SnO₂ Lattice Physics

Ambient water vapor ($H_2O$) mechanically displaces adsorbed oxygen ions ($O^-$ and $O^{2-}$) on the heated tin dioxide ($SnO_2$) crystalline lattice surface of MOX sensors. Because these sensors are fundamentally "reducing gas" detectors, this displacement causes electrons to be released back into the conduction band. This lowers the sensor's overall resistance—mimicking the exact electrical signature of a VOC gas exposure. Therefore, a sudden spike in humidity looks electrically identical to a sudden spike in ethanol, necessitating the rigorous DHT11/BME280 compensation math found in our `calibration_logger.py`.

Introduction & Motivation

Data is the lifeblood of Machine Learning. Gathering high-quality olfactory data is incredibly time-consuming due to sensor burn-in times and sample degradation. This section acts as a massive repository of 18 publicly available, pre-labeled datasets (from IEEE DataPort, Mendeley, Kaggle, and UCI) allowing researchers to immediately begin training AI models without touching a single physical sensor.
